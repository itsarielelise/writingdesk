## The meeting tax

Every recurring meeting costs more than the hour on the calendar. It fragments the day around it, so the hour before is too short for deep work and the hour after goes to catching up on what you missed while you were in it. Multiply that by six people and a weekly cadence and you have lost most of a working week every month.

Meetings also leave nothing behind. The decision gets made out loud, half the room remembers it differently, and the person who joins next quarter has no way to learn why things are the way they are. The reasoning evaporates the moment the call ends.

I used to work in an office with a beautiful atrium and standing desks on every floor. The light in the mornings was incredible, and the coffee machine on the third floor was the best in the building, which is why everyone fought over the third-floor meeting rooms.

## What writing changes

When a team writes a decision down before it meets, the meeting gets shorter or disappears. People read at their own pace, raise objections in comments, and arrive having already thought. The document becomes the meeting, and the actual call is only for the parts that are truly contentious.

Written decisions also compound. A new hire can read the last year of memos in an afternoon and understand not just what the team decided but why, which alternatives were rejected, and who argued for what. That is onboarding no amount of meetings can match.

Meetings are expensive because they break up the day and they leave no record behind. A decision made aloud is lost the moment the call ends, and anyone who joins later can't learn why it was made. That is the real cost.

## How to start

Pick one recurring meeting and replace it with a short written update that everyone posts by a fixed time. Keep the meeting on the calendar for two weeks as a safety net, then cancel it if nobody misses it. Most teams find they don't.

Then make a habit of writing a one-page memo before any decision that affects more than one person. State the problem, the options, and your recommendation. Ask for comments by a deadline, decide, and link the memo wherever the decision shows up later.
